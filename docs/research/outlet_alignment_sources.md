# Sources on the political alignment of Libyan media outlets

*Searched 15 October 2026. Ranked by how authoritative and how specific each source is. Labels are contested.
Desert Watch records them only "as published", with the source link, its date and a confidence level, and they
never feed filters automatically.*

## Usable sources that name outlets

### 1. Wollenberg & Richter (2020), *International Journal of Communication* 14: 1173–1193 — peer-reviewed
"Political Parallelism in Transitional Media Systems: The Case of Libya." Anja Wollenberg (MiCT) and Carola Richter
(Freie Universität Berlin). Licence: CC BY-NC-ND. PDF: https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/12698/2983

- **Method:** a database of 172 Libyan outlets compiled in March 2018 by local Libyan analysts for Libya-Analysis. Each outlet was coded on 13 variables, including funding, ownership and staff affiliations.
- **Coverage:** Table 1 classifies the 14 TV stations. The underlying 172-outlet database is not public.
- **Age:** the data is from 2018. One footnote says TV 218 was "rather objective" at the time of mapping but "turned clearly pro-LNA/Haftar in 2019."

### 2. Libyan Center for Freedom of Press (LCFP) with MENA Media Monitor (2023) — monitoring study
"Political propaganda and the lack of pluralism in Libyan media." Released 4 September 2023, ahead of the planned
elections. https://lcfp.org.ly/project/political-propaganda-and-the-lack-of-pluralism-in-libyan-media/?lang=en
(full report PDF linked from that page).

- **Method:** 450 hours of live broadcast across 9 channels. For six political figures, the study measured share of airtime and whether coverage was positive, neutral or negative.
- **Strength:** the most recent channel-by-channel evidence, and it is measured from content rather than asserted.
- **Limits:** it covers TV only, and LCFP is a press-freedom advocacy organisation.

| Channel (2023) | Finding as published |
|---|---|
| February | favours PM Dbeibah, "believed to own it" (44% of coverage, 91% positive or neutral) |
| Libya Al-Hadath | favours Haftar, run by one of his sons (89% positive). The report calls its bias "blatant" |
| Libya Al-Mustaqbal | affiliated with the HoR presidency, favours Aguila Saleh |
| Al-Tanasuh | affiliated with Dar al-Ifta, favours Mufti al-Ghariani |
| Al-Masar | heavily negative on Dbeibah (25% of coverage, 82% negative) |
| Libya Al-Ahrar | Haftar its most-covered figure, 59% negative |
| Al-Wasat | "no blatant bias" toward any actor |
| Al-Rasmia, Al-Watanya (public) | serve the Presidential Council and GNU, no pluralism |

### 3. State Media Monitor (2026 cycle) — state-media classification database
https://statemediamonitor.com/libya/ and https://statemediamonitor.com/2026/06/libyan-news-agency-wal/

- **LANA / WAL** is classified as state-controlled. It runs two parallel editions: Tripoli (GNU-aligned) and Benghazi (aligned with the eastern authorities). This matters for us, because lana.gov.ly content can come from either camp.
- **Unclassified:** LRTC / Al-Wataniya is left unclassified for lack of evidence.
- **Private channels:** Al-Ahrar, 218, Alassema and Libya al-Hurra are described as privately owned and politically aligned, without saying with whom.

## Context sources (no usable outlet list, or older)

- **RSF, Libya country page:** https://rsf.org/en/country/libya. Outlets are pushed to side with a party, financing comes through allied businessmen, and nobody in the east can criticise the military.
- **USIP (2015):** "The Role of Media in Shaping Libya's Security Sector Narratives." https://www.usip.org/publications/2015/04/role-media-shaping-libyas-security-sector-narratives. It covers three channels. Al-Ahrar was "most balanced, cautiously anti-Islamist", Al-Nabaa "mainstream Islamist" and Libya Awalan "strongly anti-Islamist". This contradicts the 2018 label for Al-Ahrar, which shows how quickly labels date.
- **Human Rights Watch (2015):** "War on the Media." https://www.hrw.org/report/2015/02/09/war-media/journalists-under-attack-libya. It covers attacks on journalists and notes that ownership is often unclear.
- **Media Landscapes (European Journalism Centre):** https://medialandscapes.org/country/Libya. The profile is useful for history. Its TV page returned a server error when checked.
- **Stanford Internet Observatory (2020):** "Slanted Narratives, Social Media, and Foreign Influence in Libya" (Grossman, Jonsson, Lyon, Sizer). https://stacks.stanford.edu/file/druid:hc706ht0159/20201110%20-%20libya.pdf. It scores 16,662 Facebook posts for GNA-versus-LAAF slant by where the page admins are based. That method could be reused, but the report gives no list of outlets.
- **SIO / Graphika (2020):** "Stoking Conflict by Keystroke." https://purl.stanford.edu/qg973fs1649. It covers networks linked to the Internet Research Agency that worked with LNA media operatives.
- **Stanford FSI:** "Prigozhin-linked group funding Libyan broadcast media." https://fsi.stanford.edu/news/libya-prigozhin. On Al-Jamahiriya TV.
- **Fanack (undated):** "Media in Libya." https://fanack.com/libya/media-in-libya/. A secondary summary, so use it only as a pointer.
- **The New Arab (2025):** names Al-Hadath TV as pro-Haftar. This is journalism, corroborating LCFP 2023.

## What is missing

- **Websites and Facebook pages.** No authoritative public source classifies Libyan news websites or Facebook pages. Every source above is about TV channels. Our outlets are mostly websites, such as alwasat.ly, libyaobserver.ly and almarsad.co. Only some of them are the web arm of a classified channel: libyaalahrar.tv, libya24.tv, 218tv.net, almasar, al-wasat.
- **No current all-outlet mapping.** The Libya-Analysis database behind the 2020 paper is not public. Asking the authors or Libya-Analysis for it is the best route to a wider and newer set.
- **Meta takedown reports.** These cover Russia-, UAE- and Egypt-run networks targeting Libya (2019 and 2020). We found none attributing a network to a Libyan faction.

## How these could go into Desert Watch

- **Dimension and confidence:** enter them as `alignment` categories with confidence "reported".
- **Dates:** set validity from the source date (2018 for Wollenberg & Richter, 2023 for LCFP), so the history shows how labels changed. For example, 218 moves from unclear in 2018 to pro-LNA from 2019, and Al-Ahrar has conflicting labels for 2015, 2018 and 2023.
- **Wording:** keep the source's own wording in the note, as with the scrape labels.
- **When sources disagree:** the newest content-measured source (LCFP 2023) should be shown first. Older labels stay in the history.
